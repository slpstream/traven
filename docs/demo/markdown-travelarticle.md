`ITALIAN RIVIERA`
# Corso Imperatrice, the Promenade Built for an Empress

### Faded black-and-white postcards bring back memories of old Sanremo — a sun-gilded slip of Liguria where a consumptive tsarina found solace beneath the palms and built a seafront promenade named in imperial honor.

*By Elspeth Marlowe · June 6, 2026*

SANREMO, ITALY  — The postcards are unremarkable in their anonymity: black-and-white images, worn at the corners, tucked inside a vintage album between pages belonging to somebody else's past. Yet they preserve, with quiet precision, one of the most beguiling scenes the Mediterranean coast has ever arranged for itself — the *Passeggiata Imperatrice* of Sanremo, its colonnade of towering palms casting long shadows across the promenade, the silver glint of the Ligurian Sea just visible beyond the railing. In one image, a lone figure pauses at the edge of the frame, as if uncertain whether to step into the century ahead.

==Sanremo sits at the western tip of the Ligurian arc, barely forty kilometers from the French border== — close enough that on certain evenings, with the mistral pushing from the west, one might almost mistake the language drifting up from the harbor. This is the **Riviera dei Fiori**, the *Riviera of Flowers*, the [most perfumed stretch of the Italian coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liguria), where terraced hillsides tumble with carnations, mimosa, and roses destined for the flower markets of Europe. The town exists in that luxurious ambiguity specific to borders: not quite France, not quite the Italy one expects.

## An empress beneath the palms

The promenade bears its regal name — Corso Imperatrice, Passeggiata Imperatrice — by decree of sentiment rather than any formal proclamation. ==It was in the winter of 1874 that Empress Maria Alexandrovna of Russia, wife of Tsar Alexander II and mother of the future Alexander III, arrived in Sanremo seeking relief from the tuberculosis that had been slowly consuming her.== The Riviera's reputation as a sanatorium of the soul was already well established by then; Sanremo was its jewel. The Maritime Alps, ranged behind the town like a kindly bulwark, deflected the cold tramontane winds, and the sea offered a blue horizon that seemed, at least, to hold out a promise.

[image src="https://traven.dev/img/sanremoempress.jpg" align="right" size="medium" alt="Empress Maria Alexandrovna of Russia" caption="Empress Maria Alexandrovna"]

The empress's sojourn elevated the town in ways that no modern publicity campaign could replicate. In her wake came the Russian aristocracy, the British peerage, and the Continental haute bourgeoisie, all arriving aboard the railway that had recently stitched the coast together. Sanremo grew upward and outward: grand hotels rose along the waterfront, the [Casino Municipale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanremo_Casino) opened its gilded salons, and the onion-domed Russian Orthodox church — the Chiesa di Cristo Salvatore — appeared above the old town like a small miracle of the steppes transplanted onto Ligurian soil. The Passeggiata Imperatrice itself was laid out as a suitably grand stage for this cosmopolitan parade: wide, shaded, unhurried, and very deliberately beautiful.

What the aristocrats understood, and what the vintage postcards quietly confirm, is that this corner of the Mediterranean had been arranged, almost by design, for human pleasure. The palms were imported; the pergolas were constructed; the views were framed. Even the climate felt deliberate. Sanremo records more sunshine hours per year than almost anywhere else in northern Italy, and its January temperatures — rarely falling below ten degrees Celsius — made it a credible rival to the resorts of the French Côte d'Azur, which lay just beyond the headland to the west.

## Flowers, France, and a slower future

What the postcards cannot convey is the smell. The hinterland behind Sanremo — the Ligurian hillsides climbing steeply toward the Alpi Marittime — has been given over to flowers for more than a century. Carnations especially: hundreds of varieties, from deepest crimson to the palest ivory, cultivated on terraces held together by dry-stone walls that predate the Risorgimento. The town calls itself the *Città dei Fiori*, the City of Flowers, and each spring a festival fills the Corso Imperatrice with exactly the color those grey-toned postcards were never equipped to record. The flower industry has been Sanremo's quiet economic engine for generations — one that dispatches its beauty northward to the great markets of Amsterdam and beyond.

[image src="https://traven.dev/img/sanremocasino.jpg" align="left" size="medium" alt="Sanremo Casino" caption="Sanremo Casino"]

The proximity to France has always been both Sanremo's complication and its grace. The border at [Ventimiglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventimiglia) is a twenty-minute drive; [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice) is a comfortable hour along the coastal autostrada; [Monaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monaco) floats somewhere in between, a principality unto itself. The shared geography has long blurred national distinctions: the Ligurian dialect borrows generously from Provençal, the cuisine leans toward olive oil and fresh herbs rather than butter, and the light — that particular antique gold of late afternoon on the Riviera — makes no distinction between the flags it touches. For centuries, travelers arriving from the north via Nice simply found that Sanremo was where the Riviera continued, uninterrupted, in Italian.

Today, Sanremo wears its history with studied nonchalance. ==The Passeggiata Imperatrice remains one of the most handsome seafront promenades in Italy: the palms have grown to improbable heights, the pergola of wisteria blooms violet each April, and the benches fill at dusk with the same unhurried population that has always gathered to watch the light change on the water.== The Casino still operates. [The annual music festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanremo_Music_Festival) that has borne the town's name since 1951 still holds the Italian nation in rapt, melodramatic attention each February. And somewhere in a flea market or an old bookseller's stall, another album of postcards waits, patient as the sea, to remind the next visitor that some places are simply permanent — that they exist, with quiet insistence, season after beautiful season.
